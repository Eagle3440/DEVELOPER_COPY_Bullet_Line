Bullet Line: Scripture Pursuit (KJV)

The Bullet Line

The story behind the journey
Relive Real Events — Straight from the Pages of Scripture
The Story

There is a train no one can explain.

No company built it. No rails carry it — it does not run on rails at all. There is no track under it, in your day or in theirs, and no track left behind it when it is gone. No timetable can hold it, because it does not run from place to place — it runs from now to then.

Nobody goes looking for it, exactly. It comes when it comes.

You can't summon it or schedule it. One day it's simply there, and you understand — quietly, without any announcement — that it has come for you, and this journey is yours to take. Most passengers feel wonder at that, and a little unease, and leave it at that.

If you want to know what it is, the station board already tells you. It rolls past in the lights, same as it always has:

STEP ABOARD THE BULLET LINE EXPRESS FOR A JOURNEY BACK THROUGH TIME - RELIVE REAL EVENTS STRAIGHT FROM THE PAGES OF SCRIPTURE

Most people read that as an invitation. It is more than that. It is a description — and every word of it is true.

Step aboard, because there is no other way to get there. No rails lead back. Only the Bullet Line goes.

A journey back through time, because that is what the ride is — not a trip across land, but a trip across ages, out of your own century and into theirs.

Relive real events, because the Bible tells all about them — and now you will do more than read what you have been told. You will stand in them while they happen, as they happened.

Straight from the pages of Scripture, because that is its route and its limit. It goes only where the pages go. It adds nothing that was not written, and it leaves out nothing that was. Every stop was a page first — every place, every life, every word spoken along the way.

At first, you do not believe it. No one does. You stand there curious and doubtful at the same time, telling yourself it cannot be what it looks like — and yet your heart is beating harder than you want to admit, because something in you already knows it is real.

You board in your own day. The doors close — and for one sharp second, excitement turns to fear. There is no getting off now. No turning back.

Because the Bullet Line does not leave gently.

It goes with a blast.

The lights stretch. The air tightens. The world you know falls away behind you — and with a blast, the Bullet Line is through: through the space between now and then, where the years run past like a tunnel of light. The roar presses you back in your seat and takes your breath — and that is when you learn why every passenger ends up in the window seat, face close to the glass, afraid to blink.

Because outside your window, it is not darkness going by. It is time.

Events pass through your window.

A sea splits in two and a nation walks through on dry ground — gone in a flash. Lightning cracks over a mountain while a man stands alone in the fire and smoke. A giant falls in a valley. A temple rises in gold, and falls in flame. A star hangs burning over a little town. A cross stands stark on a hill against a blackened sky. A stone moves — and dawn comes up over an empty garden.

On and on they stream past your seat, century after century of them, too fast to step into, too real to be a picture. You are seeing the whole Bible go by your window — page after page, life after life. You feel small in the best and most humbling way. Everything you thought was big about your own life shrinks, and everything you thought was distant about the Bible grows close. Some passengers cry on that ride without fully knowing why. Nobody looks away.

Then, little by little, the Bullet Line begins to slow. The events outside your window slow with it. The flashes stretch out into scenes. The scenes stretch out into a place. And the last event passing your window does not pass at all — it settles. It sharpens. It becomes a world, solid and still and waiting on the other side of the glass.

The blast dies. You are through.

That blast is why no one in history will ever hear it coming. The roar happens between times, on the way through — not in their world. So the Bullet Line arrives the way it always arrives: silent. Unseen. Unheard. Unfelt. And it can arrive anywhere — a crowded street, a hillside, a shore, a garden at dawn — because it needs no rails to be there, and the ground under it bears no mark that it ever was.

Step down, and you are standing in Bible history — not in a picture of it, not in a retelling of it, but in the living moment itself, exactly as it took place. Your disbelief is gone. What replaces it is reverence. You find yourself almost afraid to speak above a whisper.

You can stand on the slopes of Ararat as the waters go down, and feel hope come back to a drowned world. You can be at Jericho on the seventh day, when the priests lift their trumpets and the walls begin to shake, and feel the ground of God's power under your feet. You can watch the sun rise over the Sea of Galilee as the boats come in heavy with fish. You can stand on a grassy hillside and see a boy's small lunch pass from hand to hand — and never run out — and feel plain, childlike joy at it. You can be in Bethany when a voice calls into a tomb, and a dead man walks out alive, and feel shock turn to laughter and tears at once. You can come to a garden in Jerusalem at dawn, and find a stone rolled away, and a grave with nothing in it — and feel a joy so big it makes you quiet, because no words you own are big enough for it.

You will see their faces. You will hear their voices. The dust, the wind, the crowds, the fear, the wonder — all of it real, all of it happening around you.

But here is the mystery of the Bullet Line:

They will never see you coming.

No one in that world can see it. No one can hear it. No one can feel it pass.

And that is the hardest feeling of all. You will want to reach out. To warn someone. To comfort someone. To thank someone. To say, I know how this ends — hold on. You cannot. It passes through a crowded Jerusalem street and no one steps aside. It stands near Roman soldiers and not one hand goes to a sword. It is within arm's reach of kings and prophets, and no eye ever turns toward it. A shepherd does not look up. A disciple does not pause mid-sentence. History does not notice it at all — and history does not change by one word because it was there.

So the ache settles into something else: humility. You learn to do the one thing it brought you there to do.

It does not interfere. It witnesses. And so do you.

When the journey ends, almost no one is ready. You board again with gratitude and grief mixed together — grateful you were allowed to see it, grieving that you have to leave it. The ride home is quieter than the ride out. Nobody needs to talk at first.

And then the Bullet Line does the last mysterious thing it does.

It lets you go — and it goes on without you, to its next journey. It was never yours to keep. Your journey is done, and it is gone.

And you do not remember the train at all.

Try, later, and you will find it is like trying to remember a dream by morning. There was a blast. There was a window. There was light going by. But the more you reach for the train itself, the more it slips away, until there is nothing you could describe, nothing you could prove, nothing you could point to. Because it does not run on rails, it left you no way back and no way to trace it: no rails to follow, no track in the dust of their world, no track in yours.

The only thing left is a memory.

Not of the train. Of seeing it all.

The faces stay. The places stay. The voices, the words that were spoken, the things that were done — they stay, sharp and living, almost like a memory in a dream that you know was no dream at all. And every time after, when you open your Bible, the pages are never flat again. They have faces now. They have a sound. And underneath the gratitude there is a hunger that stays with you — to remember it right.

That is the pursuit.

No one knows where it came from. No one knows who sent it, or why it comes to the people it comes to.

It leaves no timetable. It leaves no rails. It leaves no train to remember.

It leaves only people who saw — and cannot forget what they saw.
